A rare label seldom occurs alone, and a classifier can recover it from the frequent labels it co-occurs with, its companions, without having learned the label itself. Existing diagnostics of this need a trained model: none says before training whether a corpus admits the failure, or what the standard aggregate hides. Macro-F1, like any measure built from per-label confusion counts, is unchanged by how a label's recall divides between occurrences with and without a companion. We introduce a within-label estimand of that division, a reading rule under which a fall in dependence counts only if companion-free recall rises, and a statistic of training labels that bounds, before training, how far reported recall can exceed companion-free recall. Across 23 corpora, 7 training objectives and five families of label-correlation mechanisms, companion-free recall falls short on all six corpora where the estimand is defined. Up to 18.4% of the tail true positives macro-F1 credits would vanish at the companion-free rate, within the training-label bound on every corpus. No long-tail objective reduces the dependence, but architecture moves it. Models whose tail score is computed from other labels' predictions are strongly dependent.

Generative recommenders decode the semantic ID of a user's next item with a language model, and post-training them with reinforcement learning (RL) after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has become standard practice. The gains are mostly reported on the first ten results, yet in deployment such models hand hundreds of candidates to a downstream ranker or reward model. We show that RL post-training has a hidden cost: it improves accuracy at the top of the list (hit@K for small K) but, on all five datasets we study, lowers the recall of the candidate list (hit@K for K in the hundreds). The model becomes a better ranker and a worse retriever. Controlled experiments trace the loss to the negative advantages that RL assigns to the candidates it samples, the rollout. The root cause is that only one next item is logged per user, so RL treats every other candidate as wrong. Yet these candidates are the model's most plausible alternatives, and often the next items of other users with similar histories. Penalising them pushes down exactly what similar users choose next, and these items drop out of those users' candidate lists. This diagnosis points directly to a remedy: out-of-rollout anchoring, a single additional loss term that preserves the SFT model's ordering of the items outside the rollout. It keeps the gain at the top and restores recall to the SFT level on all five datasets, and it is effective under other RL configurations as well. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of this recall loss: we measure it, identify its cause, and show that, once the cause is known, a simple remedy suffices.

Parallel schedules split candidates into groups and advance a fixed quota from each. GUI agents can formulate a correct immediate plan in their reasoning yet emit an action that fails to execute it, creating an execution gap. This recurring mismatch undermines reliable task completion, as a single execution error can derail a multi-step workflow. In outcome-supervised reinforcement learning, broadcasting a negative response-level advantage across all generated tokens risks penalizing valid planning together with erroneous execution. To address this credit-assignment conflict, we introduce PACT (Plan-Action Consistency Training), a training framework that combines reference-aligned plan protection and plan-aligned action repair without altering the original scalar action reward. Using annotated reference actions to identify execution gaps, PACT protects the verified plan by masking negative policy-gradient contributions across its token span. Keeping the generated reasoning unchanged as context, it then favors the reference action over the emitted erroneous action through a preference surrogate with stop-gradient pair weights. Compared with the outcome-only baseline, PACT raises AndroidWorld task success from 60.34% to 68.97% (+8.63 pp), while improving rule-based plan-action consistency on AndroidControl High from 83.71% to 89.74% (+6.03 pp). Adapting PACT to desktop agents also improves task success from 40.92% to 44.17% (+3.25 pp) on the full 369-task OSWorld benchmark. Together, these results establish PACT as an effective credit-assignment framework for bridging execution gaps and improving task completion across mobile and desktop GUI environments.

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) provide a unified interface for language-conditioned visual perception. In pixel-to-sequence grounding, autoregressive coordinate generation requires multiple sequential decoding steps per box, increasing inference cost with the number of instances. Pixel-to-embedding methods reduce this overhead but lack explicit spatial encoding when using a shared special token. We introduce SemGeo, a token-efficient visual grounding framework that represents each instance with a single grid token for coarse localization. A geometric decoder reuses visual features and hidden states to recover precise discrete box coordinates in parallel. To train proposal generation and box localization together, we introduce Gaussian soft labels that encode coordinate distances during supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Reinforcement post-training further combines box-set rewards with coordinate supervision on model-generated proposals. This stage trains the language model and geometric decoder on their own predictions to mitigate the mismatch between teacher-forced training and inference. Across eight benchmarks spanning object detection, referring object detection, and document layout grounding, SemGeo achieves the highest F1@mIou among compared MLLMs on six benchmarks. It also delivers $2.07\times$ and $1.25\times$ the mean box-generation throughput of representative grounding baselines under vLLM and Transformers, respectively, while achieving higher accuracy in both comparisons.
